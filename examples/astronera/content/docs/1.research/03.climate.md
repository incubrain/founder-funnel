## Overview

Inefficient outdoor lighting represents a triple burden: wasted energy, unnecessary greenhouse gas emissions, and exacerbated urban heat island effects. This waste is preventable through improved lighting design, technology adoption, and regulatory frameworks.

---

## 1. Energy Waste from Outdoor Lighting

  Inefficient outdoor lighting wastes 30-50% of consumed energy globally through over-illumination, poor optical design, and outdated technology.

### 1.1 Global Context

::tip
  **Key Statistics**: :cited[\$50 billion is wasted annually on light escaping to space globally]{#iea-2006-lights-labour-lost}, with 30-70% energy savings achievable through improved lighting design and controls.
::

  :cited[The International Energy Agency's landmark report "Light's Labour's Lost"]{#iea-2006-lights-labour-lost} documents that 30-50% of outdoor lighting energy is wasted through:
  - **Over-illumination** (light levels exceeding functional needs)
  - **Poor optical design** (light directed upward or horizontally)
  - **Inefficient technology** (outdated lamp types, lack of controls)
  - **Operational inefficiency** (lights operating when not needed)

### 1.2 LED Retrofit Efficiency

  Actual energy savings when replacing high-pressure sodium with LED luminaires in street lighting demonstrate 40-60% energy reductions. :cited[The Energy journal study]{#djuretic-2018-led-savings} provides empirical data on retrofit performance, confirming substantial efficiency gains.

::callout{color="info"}
  **Maharashtra Context**: Municipal street lighting energy consumption data needed for state-level waste estimates.
::

## 2. Climate Change Implications

Outdoor lighting efficiency is recognized as a cost-effective climate action strategy, with India maintaining official emission factors for calculating lighting-related GHG impacts.

### 2.1 UNFCCC Lighting Initiative

::tip
**Key Statistic**: :cited[Outdoor lighting contributes approximately 5% of global GHG emissions (~2.5 billion tons $\text{CO}_2$e annually)]{#unfccc-2023-enlighten}.
::

The UNFCCC `en.lighten` initiative promotes energy-efficient lighting as a climate action strategy. :cited[The "Momentum for Change" program]{#unfccc-2023-enlighten} recognizes lighting efficiency as a cost-effective GHG emissions reduction pathway, with outdoor lighting contributing approximately 5% of global greenhouse gas emissions.

### 2.2 India's Emission Factors

#### National Grid Emissions

:cited[The Central Electricity Authority maintains India's $\text{CO}_2$ baseline database for the power sector]{#cea-2023-co2-baseline}. These official emission factors are essential for calculating GHG impacts of lighting energy consumption.

#### Updated Emission Factors

The Universal Carbon Registry announces annual updates to India grid emission factors. :cited[The 2024 vintage]{#ucr-2025-india-grid-emission} provides current carbon intensity values for Maharashtra's electricity consumption.

### 2.3 Maharashtra Climate Impact

::tip
**Maharashtra-Specific Estimates (Preliminary)**:
- **Baseline emissions**: ~20-25 million tons $\text{CO}_2$/year from outdoor lighting (based on Maharashtra's electricity consumption share and :cited[combined margin grid factor of $0.757\,\text{tCO}_2/\text{MWh}$ for FY 2023-24]{#cea-2023-co2-baseline})
- **Reduction potential**: 1.7-4.7 million tons $\text{CO}_2$/year achievable with comprehensive dark sky policy (60-70% efficiency gains on targeted outdoor lighting sectors)
::

::note
These estimates are preliminary, derived from national-level data and typical outdoor lighting shares. Maharashtra-specific energy baseline research is needed to refine these figures. See [Research Gaps](internal:research/research-gaps#3-climate--energy) for planned research activities.
::

Maharashtra's outdoor lighting carbon footprint can be estimated using:
- State electricity consumption patterns (municipal, commercial, industrial outdoor lighting)
- Official grid emission factors from :cited[Central Electricity Authority]{#cea-2023-co2-baseline}
- Typical outdoor lighting share of total electricity demand
- :cited[Combined margin factor ($0.757\,\text{tCO}_2/\text{MWh}$) for FY 2023-24]{#ucr-2025-india-grid-emission}

Based on these factors, Maharashtra's outdoor lighting sector generates a preliminary estimate of **20-25 million tons of $\text{CO}_2$ equivalent annually**. Comprehensive dark sky regulation targeting efficiency improvements (shielding, CCT control, curfews, retrofits) could reduce emissions by **1.7-4.7 million tons $\text{CO}_2$/year**, contributing meaningfully to state climate targets.

## 3. Urban Heat Island Exacerbation

  Artificial lighting contributes to urban heat through direct heat emission, surface warming, and reduced nighttime cooling, exacerbating climate resilience challenges.

### 3.1 Nighttime Lights and Urban Heat

  :cited[NOAA's six-year assessment of major U.S. cities using multi-satellite observations]{#noaa-2023-urban-heat} documents the relationship between nighttime lights and urban heat islands. Artificial lighting contributes to urban heat through:
  - **Direct heat emission** from inefficient lamps
  - **Surface warming** from absorbed light energy
  - **Reduced nighttime cooling** in illuminated areas

::callout{color="info"}
  **Maharashtra Context**: AstronEra is conducting urban heat island research for major Maharashtra cities (Mumbai, Pune, Nagpur) to quantify the relationship between artificial lighting and nighttime temperatures. Results will inform lighting efficiency targets for the pilot program.
::

### 3.2 Climate Resilience Implications

  Urban heat islands exacerbate heat wave mortality, increase cooling energy demand, and worsen air quality. Light pollution reduction offers a co-benefit strategy: simultaneously addressing energy waste, GHG emissions, and urban heat.

## 4. Regulatory Case Studies: Energy and Infrastructure Returns

Beyond the climate science, jurisdictions that have implemented lighting regulation demonstrate measurable energy savings and infrastructure protection.

### 4.1 France: Operational Savings

::tip
**France Energy Savings**: :cited[€200 million+ annual savings via midnight curfews, 2 TWh energy, 250,000 tons $\text{CO}_2$ reduced]{#france-2018-light-nuisance-decree}.
::

France's lighting curfew regulations demonstrate substantial operational cost savings. :cited[The 2018 decree requiring commercial and public lighting to dim or switch off between midnight and 6am]{#france-2018-light-nuisance-decree} generates:
- **€200+ million annual savings** in electricity costs
- **2 TWh energy reduction** from curfew compliance
- **250,000 tons of $\text{CO}_2$** emissions avoided annually
- **No adverse impacts** on safety or security reported

### 4.2 Chile: Asset Preservation

::tip
**Chile Infrastructure Protection**: :cited[\$2+ billion astronomical infrastructure protected through light pollution regulation (30-year utility)]{#chile-2022-light-emission-standard}.
::

Chile's light emission standards protect over \$2 billion in astronomical research infrastructure (ALMA, Gemini, Magellan observatories) with multi-decadal utility. :cited[The 2022 decree]{#chile-2022-light-emission-standard} ensures:
- **Long-term asset protection**: 30+ year infrastructure lifespan preserved
- **Research capability**: Maintaining observatory dark sky access
- **International competitiveness**: Chile as global astronomy hub

These regulatory frameworks demonstrate that dark sky policy protects economic assets while generating operational efficiencies.
