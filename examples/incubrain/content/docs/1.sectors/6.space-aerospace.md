## The Legacy Projection vs The AI Reality

The standard analyst projection has the global space economy growing from $613B today to $1.8T by 2035. McKinsey published it. Consultancies are citing it. Investors are building models on it. And it will be wrong — not in direction, but in mechanism.

That projection is built on the same extrapolation that has guided space policy for sixty years: more government contracts, bigger rockets, grander missions, gradual commercialization. It treats AI as a feature add-on, not an architectural transformation. That assumption fails to account for what is actually happening at the physics layer.

The three forces that are compressing timelines in ways no legacy model anticipated:

**Launch costs dropped 95% in twenty years.** In 2000, putting a kilogram into low Earth orbit cost roughly $54,000. Falcon 9 brought that to $2,720. Starship is targeting $100/kg. That is not an incremental improvement — it is a phase transition. Every economic model that assumed launch as the dominant cost constraint of space operations has to be rebuilt from scratch.

**Smallsats replaced billion-dollar programs.** A satellite that cost $500M in 2005 is now matched or exceeded in capability by a constellation of 50kg smallsats costing $5M total. The physics of sensing did not change. The electronics did. COTS components that were too unreliable for space ten years ago are now qualified for orbital deployment, and the manufacturing volumes needed to produce them existed in consumer electronics factories the whole time.

**AI makes satellites intelligent rather than dumb data collectors.** The standard model of Earth observation: satellite captures image, transmits gigabytes of raw data to ground station, human analysts process it days later. The AI model: satellite runs onboard inference, identifies the event of interest in orbit, transmits a compressed alert with coordinates and classification in minutes. The former is a camera. The latter is a sensor. The difference is not incremental. It changes what you can charge, who will pay, and how fast you can iterate on the product.

India's space economy adds a separate acceleration layer. Currently valued at approximately $9B, the Indian government has set a target of $44B by 2033 — a 5x increase — anchored by IN-SPACe, a $119M government VC fund for space startups, and the presence of over 400 private space enterprises (up from 54 in 2020). India is not trying to replicate NASA. It is trying to build a commercial space industry on top of five decades of ISRO infrastructure, institutional knowledge, and a talent base that already produces the engineers who built half of the global aerospace supply chain from offshore locations. That is a different kind of bet — and it is one that is paying off faster than most people outside the industry realize.

## What's Actually Happening (2025–2026)

**1. Orbital edge AI is transforming satellites from data pipes into decision systems.**

The conventional satellite value chain moves data from orbit to ground before any analysis happens. Planetek Italia's third AI-eXpress satellite reached orbit in November 2025, forming the first fully operational first-generation constellation that processes information directly in orbit. Satellogic has embedded GPUs into their Earth observation satellites, running inference onboard to detect anomalies, prioritize imagery, and compress downlink requirements. China launched the first 12 satellites of its "Three-Body Computing Constellation" in May 2025 — a planned 2,800-node distributed AI supercomputer in orbit. The latency implications are significant: critical events detected in minutes, not days. The bandwidth implications are significant: transmitting a 50KB alert instead of 5GB of raw imagery. Every EO company that does not have an onboard AI strategy by 2027 will be selling a commodity.

**2. Smallsat constellations are making near-real-time global coverage a commodity.**

A decade ago, "daily revisit" over any point on Earth required a government asset. Today, Planet Labs operates a constellation providing daily coverage of the entire Earth landmass. The Pelican-2 upgrade, launching on Transporter-12 in January 2025, brought sub-50cm resolution to the daily-revisit constellation. Planet signed a $230M dedicated constellation agreement with SKY Perfect JSAT in January 2025. Pixxel completed its Firefly constellation of six hyperspectral imaging satellites in August 2025. The unit economics of smallsat manufacturing are continuing to compress, and the analytics layer sitting on top of this data is where the margin is going.

**3. Direct-to-device connectivity is eliminating the cellular dead zone.**

Starlink already delivers satellite internet to 70+ countries. Amazon renamed Project Kuiper to Amazon Leo in November 2025 and launched its first 27-satellite batch in April 2025, targeting initial enterprise service by end of 2025 and consumer rollout in 2026. More importantly, Eutelsat completed the world's first 5G Non-Terrestrial Network test via OneWeb in February 2025 — demonstrating that LEO satellites can integrate directly into 5G NR standards. The endpoint of this trend: a standard 5G smartphone with no special hardware connects directly to a LEO satellite when terrestrial cells are absent. The "connectivity everywhere" problem, which has constrained IoT deployment in agriculture, maritime, and rural infrastructure for decades, is structurally solved within 3–5 years.

**4. India's private space sector crossed the "real company" threshold.**

The numbers: 400+ private space enterprises, $617M in total funding to date, $150M raised in FY2025 alone. But the more important signal is the milestone quality. Agnikul Cosmos flew the world's first single-piece 3D-printed engine in 2024 — not a test article, an actual launch vehicle engine. Skyroot opened the Infinity Campus in Hyderabad in November 2025 — a 200,000 sq ft facility capable of producing one orbital rocket per month. Pixxel's consortium won a ₹1,200 crore government contract to build and operate India's first commercial Earth observation satellite constellation. These are not demo projects. They are commercial operations.

**5. Reusable launches are compressing the launch cost floor toward zero.**

SpaceX is targeting five times lower launch costs by end of 2025 versus early 2025 projections. Starship with full booster reuse targets $100/kg to LEO — a ten-fold reduction versus Falcon 9. Blue Origin achieved orbit with New Glenn on its first attempt in January 2025, landed the first stage on its second flight in November 2025, and announced the New Glenn 9x4 super heavy-lift variant in November 2025. Rocket Lab's Electron achieved a 100% success rate in 2025 across 21 launches with gross margins exceeding 30%. The competitive dynamics are forcing every launch provider to pursue reusability or exit the market. The floor is not yet zero, but the trajectory is clear.

**6. Commercial space stations are replacing the ISS.**

NASA's ISS decommissioning timeline creates a hard deadline. Axiom Space is targeting launch of its first commercial habitable module in 2026, docking to the ISS before undocking to operate independently. NASA has shortlisted Vast Space and SpaceX's Starship-based habitat as the front-runners for additional commercial station contracts. The addressable market: zero-gravity manufacturing, pharmaceutical research, materials science, and eventually tourism. Commercial stations are not science fiction anymore — they are the only long-term human spaceflight infrastructure plan the United States has.

**7. Orbital servicing and debris removal became commercially viable.**

Astroscale is preparing to refuel a pair of U.S. Space Force spacecraft in summer 2026 — the first commercial in-orbit refueling mission for government satellites. The on-orbit satellite servicing market sits at $4.67B in 2025 and is projected to reach $12.6B by 2035. Space debris removal is growing at 39% CAGR from a $150M base. These are not purely environmental concerns — a debris-caused Kessler cascade in certain orbital shells would destroy the economic foundation of the entire commercial space industry. Governments are beginning to pay for remediation, and the companies that prove they can dock with and service uncooperative objects will own this market for the next two decades.

## The Companies Shaping the Future

### The Launch Revolution

**SpaceX**
Starship is not just a big rocket — it is the forcing function for the entire launch cost curve. The fully reusable two-stage vehicle targets $2M–$10M per launch versus $62M for Falcon 9 currently, with a path to $100/kg to LEO at scale. SpaceX now dominates global commercial launch market share. Their vertical integration — from engine manufacturing to launch operations to Starlink as an anchor customer — means they are effectively their own launch demand generator. Every competitor is pricing against Falcon 9 today and will have to price against Starship by 2027.

**Rocket Lab**
Rocket Lab is executing the most credible "build the full aerospace stack" strategy below SpaceX. Electron serves the small satellite market with 21 launches in 2025 at 100% success rate and 30%+ gross margins. Neutron, their medium-lift reusable vehicle targeting 2026 first flight, goes after the mid-market. Their spacecraft manufacturing division builds buses for government and commercial customers. A $816M U.S. government contract locked in substantial backlog. Rocket Lab proves that a non-SpaceX launch company can build a durable, profitable business by owning a specific segment rather than competing head-to-head.

**Agnikul Cosmos (India)**
The world's first 3D-printed semi-cryo engine, launched from India's first private launch pad. Raised at a $500M valuation in 2025. In February 2026, announced a partnership with Neevcloud to build a proof-of-concept space-based AI data center to be launched by Agnibaan. Agnikul is not building a rocket company — they are building a launch platform for the Indian commercial satellite market, which needs affordable dedicated small payload launches that ISRO's PSLV manifest cannot accommodate.

**Skyroot Aerospace (India)**
India's best-funded private launch company with $99.8M raised, including $27.5M from Temasek. The Infinity Campus in Hyderabad produces one orbital rocket per month. Vikram I is targeting first flight in early 2026. The Indian government agreement for a dedicated launch facility in Telangana signals state-level commitment to Skyroot becoming a national launch provider. What Skyroot represents: the first serious attempt to build a fully commercial launch stack in India — manufacturing, integration, testing, and operations — without ISRO as the operator.

**Relativity Space**
Relativity pivoted from Terran 1 (which flew once) to Terran R — a fully reusable medium-lift vehicle with the largest 3D-printed structure ever built for a rocket. First flight is targeted for 2026. Their bet is that additive manufacturing reduces build complexity enough to compete on cost with conventionally manufactured vehicles — a thesis that remains unproven at orbital scale but is backed by $1.3B in funding and a credible technical team.

**Blue Origin**
New Glenn reached orbit on its first attempt (January 2025) and landed its first stage on the second flight (November 2025) — a two-flight reusability milestone that took SpaceX far longer. The New Glenn 9x4 super heavy-lift variant announcement signals Blue Origin's intent to compete for the largest payload class. With Jeff Bezos's capital behind it and a growing national security launch portfolio, Blue Origin is finally transitioning from "behind SpaceX" to "competing in a different segment of SpaceX."

### Orbital Intelligence

**Planet Labs**
Daily global coverage at sub-50cm resolution after the Pelican constellation upgrade. The $230M SKY Perfect JSAT agreement — a 10-satellite dedicated constellation — is a new business model: sovereign constellation-as-a-service, where a nation or large enterprise funds a dedicated orbital asset instead of buying imagery off a shared constellation. Planet has 200+ paying customers, over $200M in annualized contract value, and the largest medium-resolution daily-revisit archive on Earth. Their value is no longer the imagery — it is the time series and the analytics built on top of it.

**Spire Global**
Spire's satellite constellation collects maritime AIS, aviation ADS-B, weather data from radio occultation, and ground IoT telemetry from a single multipurpose constellation. This sensor diversification means Spire is not competing directly on imagery — they own the global data streams that are inherently difficult to replicate: ship positions, aircraft tracks, atmospheric soundings. Their Space-as-a-Service platform lets customers buy orbital data collection without owning hardware.

**Capella Space**
Synthetic Aperture Radar is the only imaging modality that works through clouds, at night, and through vegetation canopy. Capella operates the highest-resolution commercial SAR constellation in the world. SAR's penetrating properties make it uniquely valuable for maritime surveillance, infrastructure monitoring, and military intelligence — markets where the cloud coverage limitation of optical satellites is mission-critical. The defense market is paying premium for SAR data, and Capella is positioned at the top of that stack.

**Muon Space**
Muon Space is building satellites optimized for climate monitoring: wildfire detection, greenhouse gas measurement, storm prediction. Their focus on the intersection of space systems and climate data is timely — wildfire damage in the US alone exceeded $150B in 2025, and every major insurance company is looking for early detection infrastructure. Muon proves that there is a commercially viable satellite data business in climate that is not dependent on government contracts.

**Pixxel (India)**
Pixxel's Firefly constellation of six hyperspectral imaging satellites was completed in August 2025. Hyperspectral imaging captures hundreds of spectral bands versus the handful that standard multispectral cameras see — enabling detection of crop disease, water quality, mineral composition, and industrial pollution at a granularity that standard optical imagery cannot provide. Pixxel's consortium won the ₹1,200 crore Indian government contract for the first commercial EO constellation. For precision agriculture, mining, and environmental compliance applications, hyperspectral is the data format that will replace multispectral the way digital replaced film.

**GalaxEye (India)**
GalaxEye's differentiation is sensor fusion — combining optical and SAR imaging on the same satellite platform. Neither sensor alone captures the full picture: optical misses through clouds, SAR is difficult to interpret without context. Fused data gives you the interpretability of optical with the all-weather availability of SAR. This is genuinely novel at the smallsat scale, and GalaxEye is the only Indian company attempting it.

### Mega-Constellations and Connectivity

**Starlink (SpaceX)**
Over 7,000 satellites in orbit, serving 70+ countries, with revenue estimated at $8B+ annually. The direct-to-cell capability launched in limited beta — standard smartphones connecting directly to LEO without modification. The endpoint: every cellular carrier on Earth becomes a reseller of Starlink's backhaul, and the concept of "no signal" disappears. For IoT developers, maritime operators, aviation passengers, and every infrastructure application in a dead zone, Starlink is already the answer. What changes the game further: Starlink's integration with the Tesla vehicle fleet and the potential for autonomous systems to use satellite connectivity as their fallback communication path.

**Amazon Leo (Project Kuiper)**
Amazon launched its first 27-satellite batch in April 2025, renamed the project Amazon Leo in November 2025, and is targeting enterprise service by end of 2025 and consumer rollout in 2026. Amazon's distribution channel — Prime, AWS, Alexa — gives it a customer acquisition pathway that no pure-play satellite operator has. The AWS integration means Kuiper data flows directly into cloud compute infrastructure with no additional integration layer. For enterprise customers who are already deep in the AWS stack, Amazon Leo may be the default choice simply because of the purchasing convenience.

**Eutelsat OneWeb**
OneWeb's 648-satellite constellation is fully deployed and operational across Europe and the US. The February 2025 5G NTN test — the world's first successful 5G Non-Terrestrial Network connection via LEO — is the most important technical milestone in satellite connectivity since LEO broadband itself. If 5G NTN becomes the standard, satellite connectivity stops being a specialized product and becomes a feature of every 5G device. OneWeb/Eutelsat is positioned to be a standard-compliant backbone for that transition.

### India's Space Moment

**Bellatrix Aerospace**
Bellatrix builds green propulsion systems for satellites — thrusters that use non-toxic propellants and deliver higher specific impulse than conventional chemical thrusters. Their Rudra thruster is demonstrated and operational. Bellatrix has partnered with Skyroot on propulsion systems and collaborated with Dhruva Space and SatSure for integrated mission solutions. As India's satellite manufacturing scales, every satellite needs a propulsion system. Bellatrix is building the propulsion supply chain that the Indian space industry needs and that currently only foreign vendors can supply.

**Dhruva Space**
Dhruva operates the full satellite mission stack: design, manufacturing, integration, and ground station operations. They partnered with SatSure to deliver integrated Earth observation solutions. Dhruva's model — end-to-end mission service for customers who want to go to space without building a space company — addresses the exact gap that exists as Indian pharmaceutical, agricultural, and infrastructure companies begin to consider dedicated satellite applications.

**SatSure**
SatSure builds the analytics layer on top of satellite data, with a primary focus on agricultural intelligence and financial inclusion. Their core product translates satellite imagery and weather data into credit risk scores for rural borrowers — enabling banks to lend to farmers who have no credit history but whose crop yield can be monitored from orbit. This is one of the few satellite data applications that has genuine product-market fit at scale: a $200B+ agricultural credit market in India that is systematically underserved because traditional underwriting fails when borrowers have no paper trail.

**Astroscale**
Technically a Japanese company (Tokyo-listed), Astroscale is the global leader in orbital debris removal and satellite servicing. Their ADRAS-J mission demonstrated close proximity operations with a spent rocket stage in 2024. The 2026 mission to refuel U.S. Space Force satellites is the first commercial in-orbit refueling contract for government assets. Astroscale's moat: the rendezvous and proximity operations software stack needed to safely approach, dock with, and service an uncooperative object in orbit is extraordinarily difficult to develop, and Astroscale has more flight-proven experience than any other commercial operator.

**Impulse Space**
Impulse Space builds orbital transfer vehicles — essentially space tugs that pick up your satellite from the rideshare deployment altitude and deliver it to the exact orbit you need. The conventional rideshare problem: you get a cheap launch but land in the wrong orbit for your mission. Impulse solves this with a kick stage that provides last-mile orbital delivery. For the growing cohort of commercial satellite operators who use SpaceX Transporter rideshare missions as their launch vehicle, Impulse is the difference between a useful orbit and a compromise orbit.

**Axiom Space**
Axiom has the most complex task in commercial space: build the world's first free-flying commercial space station, starting from a module attached to an aging government facility. The first module targets 2026 launch. The business model is real — pharmaceutical microgravity research, zero-G manufacturing, and space tourism all have paying customers today, just not enough volume to justify a dedicated station independently. Axiom is betting that the ISS retirement timeline forces the transition, and that being first with habitable volume wins the market.

## What This Means for Founders

The space economy is a 12-layer stack, and most of the layers below the launch vehicle are genuinely open for technical founders.

**Satellite data APIs are where the SaaS business model lands in space.**

The Earth observation market is experiencing the same transition that stock market data experienced in the 1990s: raw data commoditizes, structured APIs create the business. Planet, Spire, and Capella all expose API access to their data streams. But the analytics layer — the domain-specific translation of orbital data into business decisions — is wide open. Precision agriculture risk scoring (like SatSure), supply chain resilience monitoring, illegal fishing detection, carbon credit verification, infrastructure inspection — every one of these is a repeatable SaaS product built on satellite data that doesn't require owning a single satellite. You build the analytics, someone else maintains the constellation.

**IN-SPACe compliance and licensing is a credentialing problem waiting for a solution.**

India's IN-SPACe platform has 1,200+ startups registered and 658+ applications processed. The compliance surface area is growing: launch licensing, spectrum coordination, orbital debris mitigation plans, remote sensing data policy compliance, export control for dual-use technology. Every Indian space startup needs to navigate this. No one has built the compliance infrastructure management tool — the equivalent of a SOC2 readiness platform, but for space operations licensing. This is a high-value niche: the customers are funded startups with no alternative, the regulatory complexity is high, and the incumbent solution is a law firm billing $400/hour.

**The India / Pune angle is structural, not tactical.**

Pune is home to 39 aerospace, maritime, and defense tech startups. DRDO's High Energy Material Research Lab is in Pune. The HEMRL, along with ISRO's SCL and VSSC facilities, creates a talent pipeline of engineers with hands-on experience in propulsion, materials, and embedded systems that no campus recruiting program can replicate. The government's $119M IN-SPACe VC fund is actively deploying. The iDEX scheme provides Rs 498.78 crore in grant funding for defense-aerospace startups over 2021–2026. This is the infrastructure that existed in the Israeli defense ecosystem in the 1990s before it produced Check Point, NICE Systems, and Elbit — technical talent concentrations adjacent to government R&D, catalyzed by a funding push. Founders in Pune have a proximity advantage in aerospace deep tech that no Bangalore SaaS founder can replicate by flying in for meetings.

**Earth observation analytics for agriculture and climate is the largest underserved market.**

India has 140M+ hectares of agricultural land, 600M people whose livelihoods depend on smallholder farming, and a $200B+ agricultural credit market that is systematically underserved. Satellite-derived crop health monitoring, yield prediction, and soil moisture tracking have been technically available for a decade — the gap is the last-mile analytics product that translates spectral indices into actionable decisions for a bank, an input retailer, or a state government. SatSure proved the model works. The surface area of applications — livestock monitoring, post-harvest logistics, irrigation optimization, crop insurance — is far larger than any single company can address.

**Space infrastructure software is the unsexy category with the best margins.**

Ground station network management, satellite scheduling optimization, mission planning software, constellation health monitoring — these are the products that every satellite operator needs and almost no one is building because they are not glamorous. Leaf Space, AWS Ground Station, and Microsoft Azure Orbital are addressing the infrastructure layer, but the application-specific operations software — the equivalent of Palantir for satellite constellations — does not yet exist as a standard commercial product. This is a services business that becomes a software business once the platform is built.

## The Bottom Line

The space economy is not unlocking on a linear trajectory. It is unlocking in the same non-linear way that cloud computing unlocked, except the underlying physics are more constrained and the moats are deeper. The companies that win are not the ones with the most impressive rockets — they are the ones that turn orbital data into decisions faster than the competition can respond.

For India specifically: the window between "ISRO builds everything" and "India has a mature private space industry" is open right now, and it will not stay open for long. The combination of government capital (the $119M VC fund, iDEX grants), infrastructure (IN-SPACe licensing, Vikram and Agnibaan launch vehicles), and talent (400+ companies, IIT/BITS/DRDO alumni) creates the conditions for a genuine startup cluster. The founders who move in the next two years capture the early-mover advantages in a sector where being first to demonstrate a technology in a specific market creates durable customer relationships and regulatory positioning that is very hard to displace.

The final frontier is open. The question is not whether to enter — it is which specific slice of the stack you can own before the big players notice it exists.
