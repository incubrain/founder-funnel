## The Legacy Projection vs The AI Reality

Traditional analysts have the BCI market growing from $2.6 billion today to $13.86 billion by 2035 — a 16.77% CAGR that sounds impressive until you understand what it is actually measuring. It is measuring the world as it existed before AI could decode neural signals reliably.

That world is already gone.

Pre-AI, EEG was a research tool. A $40,000 clinical-grade headset with gel electrodes, operated by a trained technician, producing data so noisy that the most common output was a probability estimate. Consumer-grade EEG — the kind you could put in a headband and sell to a general audience — was a novelty. It could tell you were stressed. It could not tell you which of two options you wanted to select. The signal-to-noise ratio problem was not an engineering problem. It was a data problem: you could not train a model to decode neural signals reliably without millions of annotated EEG sessions, and nobody had millions of annotated EEG sessions.

Foundation models change that equation entirely. The same architecture that made language models work — train on massive unlabeled data, fine-tune on specific tasks, transfer across domains — now works for neural signal decoding. InteraXon trained its Foundational Brain Model on over 80,000 EEG sessions. Synchron is training Chiral on deidentified neural data from its implanted patient population. These models do not just make signal decoding more accurate. They make it transferable: a model pre-trained on population-level EEG can be fine-tuned for an individual's neural patterns in minutes rather than months. Consumer-grade hardware becomes clinically useful. The entire cost structure of BCI drops by an order of magnitude.

Elon Musk's thesis — that AI will scale the global economy roughly 10x within a generation — is not simply about robots or software. It is about the interface layer between human cognition and AI systems. Every AI agent, every autonomous vehicle, every robotic worker operates in a world designed for human inputs and outputs. The bottleneck is not compute. It is bandwidth between biological and digital intelligence. BCI is the only technology that addresses that bottleneck directly. Musk has publicly predicted that Neuralink-style implants will replace smartphones by 2030. Whether or not that timeline holds, the directional logic is sound: a sufficiently high-bandwidth direct neural interface is worth more than any screen-based UI ever built.

The legacy projection will be wrong in the same direction as every previous forecast for transformative technology: too conservative on magnitude, too late on timing.

## What's Actually Happening Right Now (2025–2026)

**1. Neuralink is no longer a science experiment — it is a scaling operation.**

As of early 2026, Neuralink has implanted its N1 chip in 21 patients across the US, Canada, UK, and UAE. The UK expansion alone placed 7 implants at UCLH's National Hospital for Neurology and Neurosurgery between October and December 2025 — moving from "American trial" to "international program" in under a year. Patients are reporting browsing the web, playing chess, controlling robotic arms, and composing messages using only neural signals. The company raised a $650 million Series E in June 2025 and has announced plans to shift to high-volume production and near-automated surgery in 2026. The safety tracking window extends 72 months. This is not a company still proving the concept. This is a company building the manufacturing infrastructure for volume.

**2. The first FDA clearance for a BCI implant arrived — and it was not Neuralink.**

In March 2025, Precision Neuroscience received FDA clearance for the Layer 7 Cortical Interface, a 1,024-electrode flexible film implanted through a sub-millimeter incision. The clearance is for temporary use (up to 30 days) in surgical brain mapping — a deliberately narrow initial indication that establishes the regulatory pathway. The device has already been tested in 37 patients, primarily during neurosurgical procedures. What makes this commercially interesting is the data play: every brain mapping use case generates high-resolution neural data that trains the algorithms needed for the next generation of applications. Precision is using clinical deployment to build the dataset that will fund its future. That is the same flywheel that made Waymo's autonomous vehicles competitive.

**3. Apple formalized BCI as a native input category.**

In May 2025, Apple announced the BCI HID (Human Interface Device) profile — a new protocol that makes neural interfaces officially recognized input devices for iPhone, iPad, and Apple Vision Pro. Synchron's Stentrode became the first BCI to achieve native integration with Apple's ecosystem. The closed-loop system exchanges contextual information between the device and the BCI: the screen layout tells the neural decoder what targets are available, and the decoder translates neural signals into selection events. This is not accessibility as an afterthought. This is Apple's platform team treating the brain as a first-class input source. When Apple writes a protocol, every hardware company in the ecosystem is eventually expected to support it. That includes whoever makes the consumer BCI headsets of 2027.

**4. The world's first cognitive AI foundation model launched — built on real human neural data.**

At NVIDIA's GTC 2025 in March, Synchron unveiled the roadmap for Chiral, described as the world's first Cognitive AI brain foundation model. Built with NVIDIA using the Holoscan and Cosmos platforms, Chiral is designed to abstract human cognitions from large-scale neural data collected through Synchron's minimally invasive Stentrode implant. The architecture is self-supervised: the model improves as more patients are implanted, without requiring manually labeled training data. The implication is profound. If Synchron can scale implant volume — possible given that the Stentrode is inserted via the jugular vein rather than open brain surgery — it accumulates the neural dataset that no competitor can easily replicate. First-mover advantage in BCI hardware is less about the hardware than about the neural data collected through it.

**5. Adaptive DBS — the first closed-loop brain stimulation system — received FDA approval.**

On February 24, 2025, Medtronic received FDA approval for BrainSense Adaptive DBS (aDBS), the world's first deep brain stimulation system that self-adjusts in real time based on a patient's own neural signals. It serves over 40,000 existing DBS patients on Medtronic's Percept devices — making this the largest commercial deployment of BCI technology in history by any reasonable definition. Patients using aDBS in clinical trials needed lower overall stimulation energy, spent significantly more time in "on" states without troublesome dyskinesias, and reported better quality-of-life scores. This is not experimental neurotechnology. It is FDA-approved, commercially deployed, revenue-generating BCI infrastructure.

**6. Consumer BCI crossed the $500 price point with clinically meaningful signals.**

InteraXon's Muse S Athena, launched in March 2025, is the first consumer-grade wearable to combine EEG with functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS), adding blood oxygenation monitoring in the frontal cortex to conventional brainwave tracking. The underlying Foundational Brain Model was trained on more than 80,000 EEG sessions. Neurable raised a $35 million Series A and is shipping the MW75 Neuro headphones — premium ANC headphones with 12 embedded EEG electrodes that track focus, cognitive load, and burnout risk throughout the workday. These are not lab instruments. They are consumer products with professional-grade signal processing. The market for cognitive performance monitoring is real, and enterprise wellness programs are already buying.

**7. China entered the race with ultrasound-based non-invasive BCI.**

In March 2026, Gestala — a Chinese startup backed by gaming mogul Tianqiao Chen — raised $21.6 million just two months after launch, at a valuation of $100–200 million, making it the largest early-stage BCI funding in China's history. Gestala's technology uses focused ultrasound to both read and stimulate neural activity, requiring no surgery, no EEG electrodes, and no physical contact beyond the transducer. Lead applications are chronic pain and mental health conditions. The company plans to run clinical trials at 20–33% of equivalent US costs by partnering with major Chinese hospitals. Non-invasive ultrasound, if it works at the resolution Gestala claims, would leapfrog electrode-based approaches for many applications. It is worth watching closely.

## The Companies Shaping the Future

### The Invasive Pioneers

**Neuralink**
The most visible BCI company on earth. Its N1 implant — 1,024 electrodes on flexible threads thinner than a human hair, inserted by a robotic surgeon — records from individual neurons at a resolution no previous consumer-facing device has approached. With 21 implanted patients, $650M in fresh capital, and announced plans for high-volume automated surgery in 2026, Neuralink is entering the scaling phase. The current target population is people with paralysis or ALS. The long-term vision, stated plainly by Musk, is a general-purpose neural co-processor for healthy users. Whether or not that materializes on his timeline, the data Neuralink is collecting from its early patient cohort is without precedent in human neuroscience. Every competitor is working with lower resolution data.

**Synchron**
Synchron's Stentrode is the most commercially pragmatic implant in the field: it is inserted through the jugular vein, navigates the vasculature to the motor cortex, and deploys without open brain surgery. The procedure is as routine as inserting a cardiac stent, which happens millions of times annually. This scalability advantage — not the electrode count — is Synchron's real moat. More implants means more neural data. More neural data trains Chiral. A better-trained Chiral makes each new implant more valuable from day one. The NVIDIA partnership to run real-time motor inference on Holoscan and integrate with Apple Vision Pro via the new BCI HID protocol makes Synchron the most software-integrated implant company in the world right now.

**Precision Neuroscience**
The FDA 510(k) clearance for the Layer 7 Cortical Interface is the first time any company developing next-generation BCI technology has achieved full regulatory clearance in the US. The 1,024-electrode thin-film array can be placed through a sub-millimeter incision, and the temporary-implant framing sidesteps the most controversial regulatory questions about long-term brain implants. Precision's strategy — enter through brain mapping, build the dataset, expand the indication — is the most defensible regulatory path in the field. Raised $155 million total through a $102 million Series C.

**Paradromics**
Specializes in high-channel-count implants with a focus on ultra-high-bandwidth data transmission. The Connexus BCI uses 421 electrodes with an integrated wireless transmitter. The FDA approved a clinical trial for speech restoration in November 2025, targeting patients with paralysis who have lost the ability to communicate. Backed by $105 million in venture funding plus $18 million in NIH and DARPA grants. Paradromics is the most technically ambitious implant company after Neuralink, focused on maximizing the bandwidth of the neural-digital channel.

**Blackrock Neurotech**
Blackrock has been implanting humans longer than any other company in this list — its Utah Array is the research standard across academic neuroscience, and its MoveAgain system is a commercial paralysis BCI. Secured $200 million from Tether in 2024. As of 2025, expanding in-home trials where paralyzed patients live with BCI systems daily. Blackrock is the institutional memory of invasive BCI — 30 years of research, hundreds of implanted patients, and the longest clinical track record in the field.

### The Consumer BCI Players

**InteraXon (Muse)**
The Muse brand has 700,000+ users and the world's largest consumer EEG dataset. The Muse S Athena (launched March 2025) combines EEG with fNIRS in a consumer headband and is powered by a Foundational Brain Model trained on 80,000+ sessions. The new Deep Sleep Boost feature uses AI-driven neural signal models to extend slow-wave sleep. InteraXon is proving that a consumer BCI company can build a durable business on sleep and meditation coaching alone — while accumulating the training data needed to sell into clinical and enterprise markets as AI decoding improves.

**Neurable**
Neurable's bet is that the headphone is the right form factor: a device people already wear for hours daily, with EEG sensors integrated into the ear cups. The MW75 Neuro, built with Master & Dynamic, monitors cognitive state in real time and alerts users to burnout risk. The $35 million Series A (2025) validates the enterprise wellness positioning. Neurable is not trying to read thoughts — it is trying to read cognitive load, which is both more achievable and more commercially valuable to employers and productivity software companies.

**Emotiv**
The oldest commercial EEG company in the consumer/research space. The EPOC X (14-channel) and Insight (5-channel) headsets power research, gaming, accessibility, and enterprise cognitive monitoring applications. Emotiv's value is its SDK ecosystem and multi-decade dataset — the backbone for a large number of academic BCI studies. In a world where foundation models need massive training data, Emotiv's archives are a legitimate asset.

**NextMind (acquired by Snap)**
NextMind built a non-invasive BCI that reads visual cortex activity — effectively detecting where you are looking and what you are intending to select, without eye tracking hardware. Snap acquired the company and its Paris-based team to drive long-term AR research. NextMind discontinued hardware sales after the acquisition. What this tells you: the major AR/XR platforms believe BCI input will be necessary for their next hardware generation, and they are acquiring the talent now rather than building from scratch.

**Kernel**
Bryan Johnson's Kernel builds the Flow helmet — a wearable time-domain functional near-infrared spectroscopy (TD-fNIRS) system for whole-brain hemodynamic imaging. At $50,000 per unit, it is not consumer hardware. It is a research instrument targeting pharmaceutical companies, academic neuroscience labs, and mental health researchers who need brain-state data that clinical MRI cannot provide in daily-life contexts. Kernel is building the measurement infrastructure for a field that will eventually need it at scale.

### Clinical and Therapeutic

**NeuroPace**
NeuroPace's RNS System is the only FDA-approved closed-loop BCI for treating epilepsy. It monitors brain activity continuously, detects each patient's unique seizure onset signature, and responds with targeted electrical stimulation before a seizure fully develops. The company reached positive adjusted EBITDA for the first time in late 2025 — a significant milestone for a medical device company in a disease area that historically produced chronic under-investment. NeuroPace is demonstrating that closed-loop neurostimulation as a business model works, and that the revenue model (device + programming support subscription) is sustainable.

**Medtronic DBS**
The BrainSense Adaptive DBS approval in February 2025 is the single largest commercial deployment of closed-loop BCI technology in history. Serving 40,000+ existing patients on Percept hardware, the adaptive system self-adjusts stimulation parameters based on real-time neural signals, eliminating the manual programming cycles that previously required specialist clinic visits. Medtronic is proof that BCI is not a startup-only market — a $30 billion medical device company just made closed-loop neural stimulation its flagship product category.

**BrainGate**
The academic consortium (Massachusetts General Hospital, Brown University, Case Western, Stanford) has been implanting and tracking BCI patients longer than any group on earth. The BrainGate feasibility study is the largest and longest-running clinical trial of an implanted BCI. BrainGate does not commercialize directly — it produces the foundational research that validates the safety and efficacy data every commercial player points to when making the case to regulators and investors. If Neuralink's trials are racing, BrainGate is the reference laboratory.

**Cognixion**
Cognixion is building the ONE Axon-R, a non-invasive AR headset that uses EEG signals from the occipital cortex to enable near-conversational communication for ALS patients — without surgery. The FDA granted Breakthrough Device designation. A clinical trial (NCT06810219) launched in January 2025, currently enrolling up to 10 late-stage ALS patients. A separate study integrates the device with Apple Vision Pro. Cognixion is building at the intersection of the FDA's most receptive regulatory environment (severe unmet need in terminal disease) and the most accessible BCI hardware architecture (non-invasive, EEG-based).

### The Software and AI Layer

**BrainSightAI (Bengaluru, India)**
Founded in 2019, BrainSightAI has built VoxelBox — a proprietary platform for mapping neural connections using connectomics. The technology enables precise diagnosis and treatment planning for brain tumors and neuropsychiatric disorders. Raised $5 million in Pre-Series A funding led by IAN Alpha Fund, with participation from IvyCap Ventures. Deployed across 40+ hospitals in India, primarily in neurosurgery and radiology for brain tumor cases. BrainSightAI is solving the clinical workflow problem that every neurosurgeon faces: how do you operate on a brain tumor without destroying the functional tissue immediately adjacent to it? Their answer is a software platform, not a device. This is the right entry point for an Indian company in a regulatory environment that still requires navigating Medical Device Rules for hardware.

**Nexstem (Gurugram, India)**
Founded in 2020, Nexstem has built the Instinct — a cost-effective BCI headset with supporting OS designed to capture brain signals with near-medical precision at consumer prices. Raised $3.5 million, co-led by InfoEdge and Gruhas (Nikhil Kamath and Abhijeet Pai). Targeting health and wellness, human performance, learning, and entertainment. The "Neurotech Pack" model — headset plus OS plus SDK — positions Nexstem as a platform for enterprise and research customers who need BCI capability but cannot justify importing expensive hardware from the US or Europe. India's talent advantage in software makes the OS and analytics layer the most defensible part of this stack.

**OpenBCI**
The open-source hardware platform that a significant fraction of BCI research labs worldwide runs on. OpenBCI boards amplify EEG, EMG, and EKG signals at low cost, with fully open firmware and a large developer community. OpenBCI is not building toward a commercial product in the conventional sense — it is building the research infrastructure that trains the next generation of BCI engineers. If you want to understand neural signal processing without spending six figures on clinical equipment, OpenBCI is still the default starting point.

**Neurosity**
Built the Crown headset — an 8-electrode EEG device designed for software developers, with a JavaScript and Python SDK, a cloud backend for neural data processing, and pre-trained models for focus detection and motor imagery. The Crown targets a specific segment: engineers who want to prototype BCI applications without becoming neuroscientists. Neurosity announced it is open-sourcing its OS to lower the barrier further. Small company, targeted product, clear developer audience — a textbook example of how to build a BCI platform product before the market is mainstream.

## What This Means for Founders

BCI is not one market. It is five overlapping markets at radically different maturity levels, and the opportunity for a technical founder — especially one based in India — is different in each.

**The EEG data pipeline is the pick-and-shovel business nobody has built yet.**

Every BCI company — consumer, clinical, invasive, non-invasive — generates EEG or neural signal data and needs to: clean it, epoch it, extract features, label it, and format it for model training. This is expensive, repetitive, domain-specific work. The tools for doing it well at scale do not exist in a form that a non-academic team can use without significant onboarding. A developer tool company that makes EEG preprocessing and labeling pipelines fast, accurate, and accessible for machine learning teams would have customers in every company in this article. You do not need to understand neurosurgery to build this. You need to understand data engineering and be willing to spend time with neuroscientists to understand the domain-specific requirements they cannot currently explain to software developers.

**Neurofeedback protocols for cognitive performance are the recurring revenue model nobody has productized.**

Neurofeedback — training the brain to shift its own activity patterns through real-time audio or visual feedback — has decades of clinical evidence for ADHD, anxiety, sleep disorders, and peak performance. The evidence base is solid. The delivery mechanism is still primitive: you go to a clinic, you sit in a chair, you watch a video that responds to your brain state. It takes 20–40 sessions over months. The market is constrained by the cost and inconvenience of clinical delivery, not by lack of demand. A company that packages validated neurofeedback protocols for consumer EEG hardware (Muse, Neurable, even OpenBCI) and delivers them as a subscription service — with progress tracking, a clinical protocol library, and practitioner referral pathways — could make a real business before the hardware market reaches mainstream scale. This is buildable today with existing consumer hardware.

**Cognitive fatigue APIs for B2B enterprise are an obvious unbundling of what Neurable is building.**

Enterprise companies — airlines, hospitals, long-haul logistics, financial trading floors — have workers performing high-stakes tasks in cognitively fatiguing conditions. They currently have no objective signal for when an operator's cognitive state has degraded to the point of error risk. Heart rate variability gives a weak signal. Self-reporting is unreliable. EEG-based cognitive state monitoring gives an accurate signal, and the hardware to deliver it now costs under $500. A company that builds a B2B API product — headset-agnostic, integrating with existing workforce management and safety systems — for cognitive fatigue monitoring in specific high-stakes verticals (aviation, ICU nursing, long-haul driving) could generate substantial enterprise contracts without needing to build the hardware at all. The key insight is vertical specificity: the aviation buyer, the hospital buyer, and the logistics buyer have different regulatory environments, different integration requirements, and different willingness-to-pay profiles. Pick one.

**The India angle is not a consolation prize. It is the actual advantage.**

India carries roughly 197 million people with mental health disorders and 150 million with neurological conditions. It has fewer than 10,000 neurologists for a population of 1.4 billion — a specialist-to-patient ratio that makes the US look overcrowded by comparison. The standard of care for ADHD, anxiety, sleep disorders, and mild cognitive impairment in India is currently: wait months for an appointment, pay out of pocket, receive a prescription and a pamphlet, attempt to follow up in six months. BCI-adjacent solutions — EEG-based diagnostic tools, neurofeedback protocols delivered by trained practitioners, cognitive monitoring for at-risk populations — can reduce the specialist burden if they are built to integrate with the reality of Indian healthcare delivery: variable infrastructure, price-sensitive patients, overloaded clinicians. BrainSightAI has already proven that Indian hospitals will pay for well-designed neuroscience software. The opening exists.

Pune specifically has advantages in this space that are not obvious from the outside. IIT Bombay's deep tech investment ecosystem — the IAN Alpha Fund participation in BrainSightAI's round is evidence of that — is within two hours. The concentration of pharmaceutical and medical device manufacturing in Pune's MIDC zones creates a talent pool with hardware and clinical context. The city's software engineering talent is deployable on signal processing, data engineering, and clinical informatics problems that require a combination of domain knowledge and software depth. The founders building here are not competing against San Francisco teams who happen to know a neuroscientist. They are building from the center of the problem.

**Where to start if you are a technical founder with six months and limited capital:**

First, learn to work with EEG data. OpenBCI and Muse are cheap enough to buy and run experiments with. The MNE Python library is the standard for EEG signal processing. Get to the point where you can take raw EEG, apply standard preprocessing, extract features, and train a basic classifier. That capability opens every opportunity listed above.

Second, talk to the buyers, not the patients. Hospitals, enterprise wellness buyers, and research labs buy before consumers do. A pilot contract with one hospital radiology department, one enterprise with a fatigue management problem, or one academic research lab is more validating than a hundred consumer signups. The buyer development in Indian healthcare is hard but tractable — BrainSightAI proved it by signing 40 hospitals.

Third, do not build hardware. The consumer EEG hardware market is not waiting for another entrant. The software, the protocols, the APIs, the data pipelines — these are the opportunities that compound fastest, require the least capital, and can be built and tested while the hardware ecosystem continues to mature around you.

## The Bottom Line

The brain is the last interface. Everything before it — keyboard, touchscreen, voice, gesture — is an approximation of intent. When you type a message, you have already translated a thought into language, language into finger movements, and finger movements into keystrokes. Every translation loses information and takes time. A direct neural interface removes those translations. That is not a feature. It is a category change.

The technology to do this is no longer theoretical. It is deployed in 21 human skulls today, FDA-cleared for brain surgery, running in Apple Vision Pro, sold in consumer headphones, and generating the training data for AI models that will, within this decade, decode neural signals with the same reliability that large language models decode text.

The question is not whether this market is real. The $802 million in BCI investment in the first eight months of 2025 — a 443% increase over 2024 — answers that. The question is where the value accretes. The invasive implant companies will fight for the clinical and eventually the enhancement market. The consumer hardware companies will fight for attention and enterprise wellness budgets. The platform players — Apple with its BCI HID protocol, NVIDIA with Holoscan — will capture the infrastructure layer.

What is left for founders is the software layer that makes all of it work: the data pipelines, the protocol libraries, the vertical B2B applications, the clinical integration tools. This layer is large, it is currently underbuilt, and it compounds with every hardware improvement made by every company above. You do not need to build the next Neuralink to win. You need to build the product that makes neural data useful to the first million people who have a BCI device — whatever that device turns out to be.

That work starts now.
